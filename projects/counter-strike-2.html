Counter-Strike 2: A Game UX Study

image

About

This case study article was completed as an assignment for the class Games & UX, where we explored the industry practice of video games user research and design. I explored the effectiveness of Counter-Strike 2's UX and provided considerations for the developers if a game update were to be made.

Why Counter-Strike 2?

Few first-person shooters have had as significant a cultural impact as Counter-Strike, a multiplayer game series where two teams – Terrorists and Counter-Terrorists – compete in objective-based rounds to either plant/defuse a bomb (Bomb Defusal mode) or to defend/rescue a hostage (Hostage Rescue mode).

Among its many iterations, Counter-Strike: Global Offensive (CS:GO) stood out as the most influential, maintaining dominance in competitive esports for over a decade since its release in 2012 (it was also the first FPS game I ever played).

In 2023, Valve introduced Counter-Strike 2 (CS2)—a complete overhaul of CS:GO, built on the Source 2 engine. While CS2 retains the core mechanics and gameplay of the Counter-Strike experience, it introduces modernized graphics, improved game physics, and a reworked server architecture.

Given Counter-Strike’s popularity and influence on first-person shooters and esports, it only makes sense to examine the user experience (UX) design of its latest installment, CS2, to better understand how such an iconic game – that’s still one of the most-streamed esports today – has continued to deliver an engaging player experience that has fueled its success.

Core Gameplay Loop

A game’s core gameplay loop (CGL) is the foundation of a game’s experience. It’s defined as a sequence of actions players engage in over and over again in a game. The CGL typically includes a series of challenges, actions, and rewards that keep players engaged and coming back for more. A well-designed CGL balances challenge and reward, giving players clear goals and feedback that keep them satisfied and motivated.

The CGL of CS2 remains unchanged from previous Counter-Strike titles, and for good reason—it’s simple yet rewarding. Here’s a breakdown of the loop in Bomb Defusal mode:

Challenge: Terrorists must plant and defend the bomb while Counter-Terrorists must stop them or defuse the bomb

Action: Plant/defuse the bomb by killing opponents

Reward: Earn money through killing opponents or achieving the objective to upgrade weapons and equipment

Graphic credit: Samuel Jamieson

Looking at this CGL, you might wonder how players don’t get tired of its repetitiveness. I think that one of the most compelling reasons players don’t get tired of this loop is that CS2 has a high skill ceiling. As players play more and gain experience, they start to improve their aim and movements, get more familiar with the game’s guns and maps, and refine their strategy and decision-making. This ongoing progression means each round offers new opportunities to learn, experiment, and improve.

When I first started playing CS2, I was very bad. I was unfamiliar with the maps and the physics of the game’s guns.. I couldn’t aim to save my life, and it seemed like I got killed 20 seconds into every round I played. While this was super frustrating, I knew that I had plenty of room to improve and get better – it’s what motivated me to keep coming back despite the game’s repetitiveness.

Monetization Model

One of the things I appreciate about CS2 is that it’s free to play – the lack of a financial barrier is what allowed me to jump in and start playing. This approach has undoubtedly increased the size and diversity of CS2’s player base.

CS2’s free-to-play model means that players can’t buy stronger weapons or advantages in matches, meaning that the gameplay remains completely skill-based. Instead, CS2 generates revenue primarily through cosmetic items like weapon skins, cases (loot boxes) and their keys, stickers, and graffiti sprays.

Because skins, stickers, and sprays are purely aesthetic, players engage with the monetization system at their own discretion. This means that the player base isn't segmented by purchase – whether someone has a default skin or a high-tier knife, their gameplay experience remains the same. However, the allure of rare skins and community-driven trading keeps players engaged and invested in the ecosystem without pressuring them to participate.

In a review of CS2, Ed Thorn, a writer for the British video game journalism website Rock Paper Shotgun, states: “Valve's still at it with loot crates you've got to pay to open and the dodgy marketplaces that orbit it. But I wouldn't say it's super in your face, as you can choose not to engage with it if you'd like. I basically do not care for any skins or crates or anything and the game doesn't hit me with pop-ups or punish me for tuning out. I'm not saying I like the whole gambling thing and I wish it didn't exist, but you're at least free to disengage.”

Thorn’s review is a critical but fair perspective of CS2’s monetization model. He acknowledges the controversial aspects of loot boxes and skin-trading marketplaces, pointing out their similarity to gambling, but also highlights the way the game still allows players to opt out of participating in this model without their gameplay being negatively affected. As Thorn says, there are no penalties for “tuning out” of it.

Usability heuristics

Jakob Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics are a set of key design principles for creating user interfaces that are intuitive, user-friendly, and accessible, making them a good framework for evaluating the usability of video games. We’ll focus on three of these heuristics that play a crucial role in delivering a smooth and engaging player experience in CS2.

Visibility of System Status

“The system should always keep users informed about what is going on, through appropriate feedback within reasonable time.”

One of the most important aspects of any game’s UX is making sure players are constantly aware of what’s happening – it’s especially important for an FPS game like CS2, where players need clear, immediate feedback to make decisions in a fast-paced, high-stakes environment.

Notice in the image above the heads-up display (HUD) in CS2 that clearly shows the player all the necessary stats, like armor strength and health on the bottom left, bullet count on the bottom right, and the players remaining at the top. All of this information is instantly updated during gameplay, providing helpful visual feedback for the player to determine their next action. For example, if I’m hit by an opponent and my health drops, I’ll probably play more cautiously and peek less; I might decide to hold a defensive position rather than keep pushing out to engage with other opponents. I also have a tendency to excessively reload my gun, so I’m always keeping an eye on the bullet count to let me know when I should do so to prepare for future gunfights.

Match Between System and Real World

“The system should speak the users' language, with words, phrases and concepts familiar to the user, rather than system-oriented terms. Follow real-world conventions, making information appear in a natural and logical order.”

As a tactical first-person shooter, users expect a certain degree of realism from CS2 in terms of weapon functionality, strategy, and objectives. CS2 has done a great job at ensuring this and creating a match between the game and the real world.

The image above highlights how CS2 adheres to this heuristic – the game includes weapons inspired by real ones, like the AK-47, M4A4, SSG 08, and Desert Eagle. Aside from their realistic visual appearances, the game’s guns also mimic the recoil and accuracy of how they behave in reality. With other utility items, players who are familiar with real-world physics can predict how grenades will behave, making strategies like bouncing a smoke off a wall or timing a flashbang feel intuitive. By ensuring that in-game mechanics align with real-world expectations, CS2 makes it easier for players to understand weapon behavior without needing a tutorial.

Recognition Rather Than Recall

“Minimize the user's memory load by making objects, actions, and options visible. The user should not have to remember information from one part of the dialogue to another. Instructions for use of the system should be visible or easily retrievable whenever appropriate.”

Helping users recall information through visible cues rather than expecting them to remember every detail speeds up interactions and makes the player experience less frustrating, especially in a game like CS2 where there’s so much going on at all times.

The image above demonstrates how CS2 does a good job of reducing the player’s cognitive load by presenting context-relevant keys on the bottom of the screen for actions players can take when they’re in the buy menu at the start of a round. This speeds up the buy process, which is important given the short buy period. By also providing this information only when it’s relevant, CS2 keeps the user interface clean and focused while still offering guidance when the player needs it.

A different lens

Overall, CS2 demonstrates strong adherence to these three Nielsen heuristics—keeping players informed in real time, aligning game mechanics with real-world expectations, and reducing cognitive load through clear visual cues. Together, these principles form the foundation for a smooth and intuitive gameplay experience for players.

While Nielsen’s heuristics are widely used for evaluating usability and are easy to apply because of their generality, they were originally developed for traditional user interfaces rather than video games. CS2 performs well when assessed through these broad UX principles, but what if you took a closer look at it using heuristics developed specifically for evaluating video games? Would the user experience still hold up under a more specialized lens?

To answer this, we turn to GAP heuristics, specialized guidelines for designing an intuitive and engaging initial user experience for new players. This is especially relevant for CS2 given common community feedback about how difficult it is to get into for new players. As Jake Tucker puts it in his review on TechRadar, “Counter-Strike 2 is an exceptional game. But it’s impenetrable and hostile to newcomers…”

Gap heuristics

Game Approachability Principles (GAP) are a set of 27 heuristics broken up into 4 categories that provide a framework to help game developers create good experiences for new players. We’ll examine three of them below.

“The game is similar to other games of a similar genre and platform and games in the same series (Category: Knowledge Transfer).”

By maintaining consistency with other games in the same genre or series, developers reduce the learning curve, allowing players to make a smooth transition without having to learn fundamental mechanics from scratch, resulting in a more intuitive game for new players and players who have played a previous installment of a series.

CS2’s gameplay mechanics remain virtually unchanged from its predecessor CS:GO, the series’ most popular and well-known installment. This consistency in mechanics allows players who have played the previous entry to transfer their knowledge and skills seamlessly into CS2. When I played CS2 for the first time after having played CS:GO for many years, I found that not much had changed. The shooting mechanics, movement, and gameplay are the same as CS:GO, allowing me to transition to CS2 pretty seamlessly.

As an FPS game, CS2 maintains many mechanics similar to those found in other games of the same genre. For example, FPS games similar to CS2, like Valorant and Call of Duty all allow players to cycle through their weapons using their mouse wheel or keyboard. In all three games, players can right-click on their mouse to aim down sights for more accurate and precise shooting.

“The game allows players to practice in different ways (Category: Demonstration & Practice).”

Players, especially new ones, should have multiple opportunities to learn and refine their skills. Instead of being dumped into a single, high-stakes environment, players should be offered multiple, low-stakes modes to practice in before experiencing gameplay that has real consequences.

CS2 does an excellent job of providing players with multiple game modes to explore and get used to gameplay and mechanics, as shown in the image below. By allowing players to practice in different modes, new players are less likely to be frustrated because they can learn the game without the pressure of ranked play. Different practice options also allow players to focus on specific skills that they might not be able to if their options were limited to competitive play.

“The player is able to practice new actions without severe consequences (Category: Demonstration of Actions With Feedback).

On a similar note, while it’s important that a game gives players different modes to practice in, it’s equally as important that players can try things without consequences.

CS2 does a great job adhering to this heuristic by providing players with a highly customizable practice mode. Players can choose from a multitude of maps and adjust settings to how they like. In practice, players can try new things and learn new skills without consequence, unlike other modes like Casual or Deathmatch, where there may not be severe consequences, but players can still lose. I’ve personally utilized this feature many times to test out grenade lineups and practice peeking corners and work on my aim, all without the pressure of a real game.

These three heuristics highlight how CS2 succeeds in providing a welcoming and supportive experience for new players by thoughtfully balancing familiarity with opportunities to practice and experiment safely. Through design choices that respect players’ prior knowledge while encouraging skill development without harsh penalties, the game creates an engaging entry point that helps ease the learning curve.

Overall, my evaluation of CS2 using both Nielsen’s principles and GAP heuristics reveals that the game delivers a solid user experience, from both a broader usability perspective and under closer, more specific scrutiny, highlighting Valve’s effective design for a complex, competitive game.

Some considerations for developers

CS2 offers clear feedback and intuitive design for experienced players while also providing thoughtful support for newcomers through consistent mechanics and safe practice modes. However, I believe there's still room for improvement; below are two considerations developers could take into account to take the game to the next level:

1

Improved onboarding

With a game like CS2, it’s expected that players will have to put in many hours to develop their skills, but Valve developers could reduce the learning curve by improving the game’s onboarding and tutorial beyond the current basics. Developing a more interactive tutorial with progressive difficulty would help players gradually learn and familiarize themselves with the game's mechanics. The tutorial could cover everything from movement mechanics, weapon recoil patterns, to strategic positioning, which would make CS2 more approachable and foster long-term engagement.

2

Smarter practice modes

While CS2 already allows players to experiment in custom practice modes, these sessions could be made adaptive, providing personalized feedback and drills based on a player’s weaknesses. For example, this adaptive system could track aim consistency or recoil control and then generate scenarios to help players improve in those areas. This would make practice both more efficient and engaging, giving players a clearer sense of progression outside of matches.

Wrapping it up

This case study article was fun to write and was a great chance for me to analyze a game I love on a deeper level. I was able to strengthen my understanding of the principles behind well-designed player experiences, which will help inform my approach to designing future games.

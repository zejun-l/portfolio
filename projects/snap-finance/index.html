Featured Project

Spending Power Education

New and returning customers can receive an estimated Spending Power amount that represents how much they may be able to spend with Snap at participating stores.

Customers can also see how that amount differs by financing option and open an education screen to learn more. The team wanted to know whether that content actually helped customers understand what Spending Power was and how to use it.

Problem

Customers could see their Spending Power. But did they know what it meant?

Spending Power is a complex financial concept that Snap has been struggling to communicate effectively. Customers often interpret it as a loan, credit amount, spending limit, approved amount, or something else entirely.

I wanted to understand the mental model customers started with, how the education experience changed that understanding, and what questions were still left unanswered.

Research goals and methodology

What I needed to learn

  • First impression: What did customers think Spending Power meant when they first saw it?
  • Education: Did the education experience help build a clearer understanding?
  • Financing options: Did customers understand why different financing options showed different amounts?
  • The estimate: Did customers understand that their final Spending Power could change?
  • Open questions: What did customers still want to know before shopping?

Research approach

I ran an unmoderated usability study with 15 participants through Askable. The study combined prototype tasks with open-ended questions about what participants thought the information meant.

Since I was testing comprehension, I asked participants to explain Spending Power in their own words before and after exploring the education experience.

  1. Explore the initial experience
  2. Explain Spending Power
  3. Review the education screen
  4. Explain it again
  5. Interpret the financing breakdown
  6. Open questions

Synthesis

How I analyzed the responses

I coded participant responses by question, grouped similar answers, and looked for patterns across the study. I then compared those patterns against the research questions to identify the findings that mattered most.

  1. Participant response
  2. Code
  3. Theme
  4. Finding

Findings

The education improved understanding, but some questions remained

Initial mental models of Spending Power varied

Participants understood that Spending Power was connected to financing a purchase, but described it in different ways. Some saw it as a loan, credit amount, spending limit, approved amount, or estimate.

“Spending power is how much credit you can get" (P1).
“[It's an] estimated amount for you to use on the purchase” (P10).

Interest and fees were still unclear

Participants naturally connected Spending Power with the cost of financing and wanted to know more about interest and fees. When that information wasn't available, some participants filled the gap with their own assumptions.

“I would want to know the interest on the loan over the 12 months” (P15).
“What [are] the interest percentages on any borrowed money?” (P11)

The education experience improved overall understanding

After reviewing the education content, participants generally had a clearer understanding that Spending Power was an estimate and that the amount could differ by store or financing option.

Before: “It's an amount that you get financed for based on the amount wanting to be spent” (P7).
After: “It's the amount that is given to you based on a few different factors. Based off credit and amount being spent and where” (P7).

Some users misinterpreted the financing breakdown

A small group interpreted the lease-to-own and installment-loan amounts as combined, sequential, or as the amount borrowed compared with the amount repaid.

“I can spend $5,000 first and receive another $3,500 afterward.” (P3).
“It feels like I have $3,500 to spend and will have to pay back $5,000 which would be a 30% interest” (P11).

The estimate did not significantly reduce shopping confidence

Most participants said they would continue shopping even if their final Spending Power changed. Many expected some variation or wanted to see the final amount before deciding. A large or unclear change was more likely to create hesitation.

“I would use it because I would expect it to change and be based on different factors” (P12).
“It would depend on how much the added costs would be if they are much higher than I originally thought I probably would not continue with the loan as I might not be able to afford a higher payment” (P2)

Recommendation

Give customers an easier way to understand financing costs

Add interest and fee information to the FAQ: Interest and fees were the clearest unanswered questions in the study. I recommended adding this information to the FAQ so customers could find it without adding more content to the main education experience.

Outcomes

What the study answered

The education experience helped customers build a clearer understanding of Spending Power and showed that the estimated nature of Spending Power wasn't the main source of hesitation. Instead, financing costs remained a key information gap, which gave the team a clearer picture of what the education experience was already doing well and where customers still needed more information.

Project two

Servicing Messages

Returning customers may see messages about their account and payment status when they return to the Snap Finance app after making a purchase.

I ran two unmoderated usability studies with 30 total participants: one focused on urgent header alerts and the other on lower-priority banners.

The goal was to understand whether customers noticed the messages, interpreted them correctly, understood the intended urgency, and knew what to do next.

Active header alerts

Urgent payment alerts were clear and noticeable

The alert was understood as payment-related and urgent

Participants generally understood that a payment was due or past due. The red color, bold styling, and placement at the top of the screen helped the message stand out and communicate that action was needed.

“I feel like it was very urgent because it was at the top of the screen and highlighted in bright red” (P4).

The alert also shaped how customers viewed Snap

Participants saw the alert as evidence that Snap was proactive about communicating important account and payment information.

Overall, this created a neutral to positive impression of the brand.

Passive banners

The banner communicated the topic, but not the account status

Customers read the banner as a payment reminder

The banner was meant to confirm that a payment had already been scheduled. Instead, participants often described it as an upcoming due-date reminder.

They understood that the message was about a payment, but not the exact status it was trying to communicate.

Shopping content drew more attention

Spending Power and shopping categories stood out more than the banner. Its lower visual salience made it easier to overlook unless participants were closely scanning the screen.

In first impressions, shopping opportunities were mentioned 14 times, Spending Power 10 times, and the payment banner 5 times.

“[The banner] didn't catch my eye initially” (P13).

Payment status raised questions about Spending Power

Some participants wondered whether a missed or past-due payment would affect their available Spending Power and ability to keep shopping.

Recommendation

Make positive account updates easier to notice

Increase banner salience and use green for positive account statuses: I recommended changing the banner treatment to green to help it stand out and reinforce that the message represented a positive account status, such as a payment already being scheduled.

Outcomes

What the study answered

The studies validated that the active header alert could communicate urgent payment information effectively.

They also identified a comprehension and visibility risk in the passive banner, giving the team direction for strengthening its visual salience and making account status clearer.

Project three

rastrack

Research recommendations can become difficult to follow after a readout, especially when their status is spread across different slide decks and conversations.

I noticed that gap during my internship and explored how a centralized tool could make recommendation adoption easier to track.

The problem

There was no central place to see whether a recommendation had been adopted, committed, communicated, or canceled.

The concept

I used Nielsen Norman Group's Recommendation Adoption Score as a starting point and translated it into a workflow for tracking recommendations, status, impact, and adoption trends.

What I did

I identified gaps in the existing process, defined the product requirements, designed the interface, and built a working web prototype. I also used AI tools throughout the design and development process.

What rastrack could help make visible

The goal was to make research impact easier to track over time: where recommendations stalled, which high-impact items needed follow-up, and how much research work ultimately made its way into the product.

Unlike the usability studies, rastrack was an exploratory side project, so I treat these as potential outcomes rather than measured impact.

Takeaways

This internship taught me how research fits into day-to-day product decisions. I learned to start with the decision a team needed to make, then design the study around the evidence they needed.

I also became more comfortable with qualitative analysis. Coding helped organize participant feedback, but the harder part was deciding which patterns mattered and turning them into a clear story for stakeholders.

Working across product research and rastrack also changed how I think about research impact. A study does not end when the readout is presented. What happens to those recommendations afterward matters too.

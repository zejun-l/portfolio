Internship overview

From research question to readout

During my summer at Snap Finance, I worked on three projects across customer financing, account servicing, and research operations.

I led three unmoderated studies with 45 participants, taking each from planning through analysis and readout. I wrote research plans and tasks, built studies in Askable, analyzed responses, and shared findings with product and design partners. Alongside my research work, I designed and built a tool for tracking what happens to recommendations after a study ends.

Featured project

Spending Power Education

Customers could see an estimated Spending Power amount before shopping with Snap. They could also view how that amount differed across financing options and open an education page for more information.

The team wanted to know whether customers understood what those numbers meant before they started shopping.

Customers could see their Spending Power. But did they know what it meant?

Spending Power could easily be mistaken for a credit limit, loan amount, or guaranteed approval. I wanted to understand the mental model customers formed when they first saw it, whether the education page changed that understanding, and what questions were still left unanswered.

What I needed to learn

  • First impression: What did customers think Spending Power meant when they first saw it?
  • Education: Did the education page change or improve that understanding?
  • Financing options: Did customers understand why they saw two different amounts and that they were alternatives rather than amounts to combine?
  • The estimate: Did customers understand that their Spending Power could change?
  • Missing information: What did customers still want to know before shopping?

Research approach

I ran an unmoderated usability study with 15 participants through Askable. Participants moved through the prototype and explained what they thought Spending Power meant before and after viewing the education page.

Because I was testing comprehension, I focused on how participants explained the experience in their own words rather than whether they could simply complete a task.

  2. First interpretation
  3. Explore the education page
  4. Explain Spending Power again
  5. Review the financing options
  6. Discuss the estimate and remaining questions

How I analyzed the responses

I coded responses by question, grouped similar answers, and looked for patterns across participants. This helped me separate isolated confusion from issues that appeared throughout the study.

Findings

The education helped, but some questions remained

Customers better understood the financing options

After viewing the education page, 11 of 15 participants understood that the amount available could differ by financing option. 12 of 15 correctly interpreted the financing breakdown.

The education helped most participants understand that the two amounts represented different options rather than money they could combine.

Most understood that Spending Power was an estimate

13 of 15 participants correctly understood that their Spending Power could change.

Customers generally expected some variation, suggesting that the estimate itself was not a major source of confusion.

Financing costs were the biggest unanswered question

8 of 15 participants asked about interest, rates, or fees.

Even when customers understood how much they might be able to spend, many still wanted to know what using that financing would cost them.

A few customers still misread the breakdown

3 of 15 participants interpreted the two financing amounts as combined, sequential, or as the amount borrowed versus repaid.

The issue affected a smaller group, but showed that the relationship between the two options could still be clearer.

The estimate had little effect on shopping confidence

10 of 15 participants said knowing their Spending Power could change would not affect their decision to continue.

For participants who were less confident, the concern was often tied to how much the amount might change or what the financing would cost.

Customers started with different mental models

Before viewing the education page, participants described Spending Power as a loan, credit, spending limit, approval, or estimate.

The education brought most participants closer to the intended understanding, but their starting points varied.

What I recommended

01

Add interest and fee information to the FAQ

Financing costs were the clearest information gap. I recommended addressing interest and fees in the FAQ so customers could answer those questions without adding more information to the main experience.

02

Clarify the financing breakdown

A few participants still misunderstood how the two amounts related. I flagged the breakdown as an area to keep refining and testing.

What the study gave the team

The study showed that the education page helped customers understand Spending Power and that being shown an estimate was not the main barrier we expected.

It also surfaced a different question: customers wanted to understand the cost of using their financing. This helped the team focus on the information customers still needed rather than adding more explanation around the estimate itself.

Project two

Servicing Messages

Returning customers may see messages about their account or payment status when they open the app.

I ran two studies to test different levels of urgency: active alerts for issues that needed attention and passive banners for routine account updates. I wanted to know whether customers noticed each message, understood what it meant, and knew whether they needed to act.

Two studies, two levels of urgency

Active header alerts

The urgent alert worked well. 11 of 15 participants recognized that it was related to a payment, and those participants consistently saw it as urgent. Its placement at the top of the screen, red color, and stronger visual treatment helped it stand out.

7 of those 11 participants said they would take action right away.

Passive banners

The passive banner had the opposite problem. Only 4 of 15 participants mentioned it while first exploring the screen. Shopping content and Spending Power drew much more attention.

Even when participants noticed the banner, they often read it as a payment reminder or upcoming amount due rather than confirmation that a payment was already scheduled.

Different messages need different levels of attention

The two studies showed that account messages could not all use the same approach. Urgent alerts benefited from strong visual hierarchy because customers needed to act. Routine updates needed enough emphasis to be noticed without making them feel urgent.

For the scheduled-payment banner, I recommended increasing its visibility and using green to better communicate that the payment had already been scheduled.

Project three

RAS Tracker

Research recommendations can be difficult to follow once a study and readout are over. I saw an opportunity to make that follow-through easier to track, so I started RAS Tracker as a side project during my internship.

The gap

There was no central place to see whether recommendations had been adopted, committed, communicated, or canceled. That made it harder to understand what happened to research after findings were shared.

The idea

I used Nielsen Norman Group's Recommendation Adoption Score as a starting point and designed a web tool that could track recommendations, status, impact, and adoption trends across studies.

What I did

I defined the product requirements, designed the interface, and built a working web prototype. I used AI throughout the design and development process to help me explore ideas and build faster.

Reflection

This internship changed how I thought about the relationship between research and product decisions. A useful study does more than identify usability problems. It helps a team understand what matters, why it matters, and what they should consider next.

I also became more comfortable with qualitative analysis. Coding helped me organize what participants said, but the harder part was deciding what those patterns meant and which ones were strong enough to act on.

RAS Tracker pushed that thinking one step further. Research does not stop when the findings are presented. What happens to those recommendations afterward matters too.

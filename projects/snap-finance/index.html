Internship overview

From research question to readout

During my summer internship, I led research across three projects: customer financing information, account-update communication, and research recommendation tracking.

My work included unmoderated usability testing, qualitative analysis, and synthesis. Across two evaluative studies, I worked with 45 participants. I owned the research process: I shaped the questions, wrote the plan and tasks, set up studies in Askable, reviewed responses, coded the data, synthesized findings, and presented recommendations. I also built a working prototype for a research-operations tool.

Featured project

Spending Power Education

Customers could see an estimated Spending Power amount: what they may be able to spend at participating stores. They could expand the amount to see financing alternatives and an education screen. The concept was easy to mistake for a credit limit, a loan amount, an approval, or a guarantee.

Customers could see their Spending Power. But did they know what it meant?

If people treated the amount as guaranteed, they could form the wrong expectation before checkout. I needed to understand their first interpretation, what changed after the education experience, and which questions remained unanswered.

What I needed to learn

First impression

How did people interpret Spending Power before they saw more context?

After education

Could they explain the amount and use it in a shopping decision?

Financing alternatives

Did they understand that the options were alternatives, not amounts to combine?

An estimate, not a promise

Did they understand that availability could vary?

Open questions

What information did they still need before feeling confident?

Research approach

I ran one unmoderated usability study with 15 general-population participants through Askable. Participants moved through a prototype, then explained the experience in their own words before and after exploring the education content.

  1. Initial experience
  2. Initial interpretation
  3. Explore the education screen
  4. Explain Spending Power again
  5. Review the financing breakdown
  6. Consider the estimate and remaining questions

How I analyzed the responses

I coded participants’ explanations, grouped similar responses, and traced each pattern back to the research question. This made it possible to distinguish a broad understanding problem from a smaller but important set of unanswered questions.

  1. Participant response
  2. Code
  3. Pattern
  4. Finding

Findings

The education worked, but it did not answer every question

Most people could distinguish the financing options

11 of 15 participants understood that availability could differ by financing option. 12 of 15 correctly interpreted the financing breakdown.

Most people understood the estimate could vary

13 of 15 correctly understood that Spending Power was an estimate, not a guarantee.

Cost was the main remaining question

8 of 15 participants asked about interest, rates, or fees after the education experience.

A small group still misread the breakdown

3 of 15 participants read the alternatives as combined, sequential, or as a borrowed-and-repaid amount.

The estimate rarely changed shopping confidence

10 of 15 said that knowing the amount could change would not affect their decision. Others tied their confidence to the size of a change or to cost and repayment questions.

Initial mental models varied

Before education, people described Spending Power as a loan, credit, spending limit, approval, or estimate.

What the team could take forward

01

Include interest and fees in the FAQ

Cost information was the clearest unresolved need in the study. The documented recommendation was to address interest and fees directly in the FAQ.

02

Keep testing the financing breakdown

The study showed that a small group still formed the wrong model of the alternatives. That gave the team evidence to revisit how the comparison is explained.

What this study gave the team

The study provided evidence for a product and design decision. It showed where education improved understanding and where people still needed clearer information. I do not claim that these changes shipped or produced a business result.

Project two

Servicing Messages

I studied two message types: active header alerts for urgent account or payment situations, and passive banners for account updates such as a scheduled payment. The goal was to see whether people noticed the message, understood it, felt the intended urgency, and knew what to do next.

Two studies, two levels of urgency

Active header alerts

In an unmoderated study with 15 participants, 11 of 15 identified the alert as payment-related. All 11 rated it as highly urgent. Red, bold styling and top placement helped communicate urgency. 7 of 11 said they would act immediately.

Passive banners

In a separate 15-person study, only 4 of 15 mentioned the banner during their initial exploration. Participants noticed shopping opportunities 14 times and Spending Power 10 times. None described the banner as confirmation that a payment was already scheduled.

Design implication

Urgency needs a different visual hierarchy from confirmation. The documented recommendation for the scheduled-payment banner was to use green and increase its salience so it reads as confirmation rather than a payment reminder.

Project three

RAS Tracker

After a study ends, recommendations can become scattered across decks and conversations. I noticed that there was no central way to see whether recommendations were adopted, committed, communicated, or canceled.

The gap

Research recommendations lacked one shared place for status, ownership, and follow-through.

The concept

RAS Tracker was inspired by Nielsen Norman Group’s recommendation adoption score and adapted it into a tracking workflow.

My contribution

I defined the concept, translated the framework into product requirements, designed the interface, and built a working web prototype with AI as design and development support.

Reflection

Working in a product environment taught me to frame a research problem around the decision it needed to inform. Constraints matter, and a useful readout explains the reasoning behind a recommendation.

I also learned that analysis requires judgment. Coding can organize responses, but the researcher still decides what the pattern means, what evidence is strong enough, and what should remain an open question.

Building the RAS Tracker changed how I think about impact after a readout. AI helped me explore and build faster, but the direction, tradeoffs, and judgment stayed human.
